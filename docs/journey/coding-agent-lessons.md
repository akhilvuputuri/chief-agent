# Coding-agent engineering: changes, failures and evidence

Updated: 8 October 2026. Baseline: integrated main `135e9898db15a19dc602ba04d03e42e970b174cf`, containing the verified v0.3.51 activation. This is a cumulative learning guide; dated journal entries retain detailed incident, review and release evidence. A healthy release does not by itself establish better coding quality.

## How to read the evidence

For each change, ask what failed, why that mechanism was suspected, what changed, which failure it actually prevented, and what would demonstrate improved engineering outcomes. Keep failed approaches and review corrections; a history containing only successful releases hides useful lessons.

- **Observed:** production metadata or CI results, with workload/window and limitations.
- **Reproduced:** a synthetic failure and correction exercised independently; proof of that mechanism, not model quality.
- **Deployed:** exact release receipt plus the stated configuration/health verification.
- **Unmeasured:** real-task success, quality, efficiency or cost impact without a comparable trial.

## What the incidents taught

### Capacity and useful progress are different

On 8 October, one resumed DeepSeek V4.1 Flash planner completed forty calls without a plan. A later fresh attempt admitted 139 calls over approximately 24 minutes: 138 complete and one uncertain. It still produced no acknowledged plan or changed file. These were different attempts, not a controlled before/after experiment. More admitted calls do not prove efficiency or quality gains.

The forty-call attempt reported USD 0.0210415264 model usage plus USD 0.03 estimated compute: approximately USD 0.051 combined. This was a read-heavy, cache-using workload, not an invoice or a forecast for coding/reviewer work. Chief chat, fixed hosting, credits, tax and ancillary charges were excluded. [Journal 70](70-coding-allocations.md#evidence-and-cost-scope) records the date, token/cache counts and pricing basis.

**Lesson:** support task duration, retain useful work and classify stopping reasons. Low observed cost is not evidence that extending an unproductive loop will complete it.

### Reading information is not retaining it

The later attempt made 237 reads across 58 files. It repeated 114 path/offset pairs after each previous result ID had left the request. Page sizes varied, so these were not necessarily identical complete ranges. This supports the context-loss hypothesis but does not prove every repeat was unnecessary or caused solely by trimming.

The old implementation deleted old groups without preserving findings. Planning checkpointed at initialization/completion, leaving a stored zero tool count despite activity. V2 saves working state during inspection and acknowledges a replacement notebook before removing older groups.

**Lesson:** externalize subtask, evidence references, unresolved questions and next action. A heartbeat, acknowledged progress and verified completion are three different signals.

### Tool ergonomics matter

The earlier leader could list paths with `rg --files`; that is not content search. Missing files yielded opaque errors. The later incident recorded 24 file-read errors. Grep/glob, clear character-versus-line cursors, fingerprints and actionable errors address those observed interface gaps. Their success-rate or cost impact still needs comparable trials.

**Lesson:** inspect actual schemas/results before adding retrieval infrastructure. Having a reader does not establish effective navigation or useful feedback.

### Recovery is a side-effect decision

A failed generation may have executed no tool; a failed command/write may already have had an effect. V2 permits one fresh generation after recognized transient model failures, while uncertain commands/writes are not blindly replayed. Working notes cannot change the approved plan, mark checks passed or substitute for review. Failed/lost generation cost can remain unrecorded after partial output; unknown accounting is not zero.

**Lesson:** save acknowledged state, classify failure and retain scope/cancellation boundaries; do not retry every exception indiscriminately.

## How future changes enter the record

Append a dated case here in the same PR as meaningful coding-harness changes, linking the detailed journal. Include:

1. **Trigger/result:** sanitized failure and expected outcome.
2. **Baseline:** exact source/image, workload, models, effort, allocation and relevant tool/context settings.
3. **Observation:** dates, units, denominators and what tests/ledger establish.
4. **Hypothesis:** mechanism and credible alternatives.
5. **Intervention:** behavior changed and its rationale.
6. **Counterexample/review:** what broke the first approach and the correction.
7. **Outcome:** reproduced behavior or real-task result, cost/latency/accounting limits and unresolved failures.
8. **Release/next test:** exact approval/deployment evidence or pending boundary; the smallest experiment that could falsify the benefit.

Keep negative/mixed results. Do not relabel implementation as demonstrated benefit after deployment.

For comparisons, follow [the benchmark contract](../../evals/coding/README.md): freeze workloads, vary one factor, hold models fixed for harness trials and harness fixed for model trials, retain infrastructure failures and grade independently. Add a realistic planning/navigation case to the small repair pack. Measure completion, time to first durable finding, repeated unchanged evidence, summary recall, recovery outcomes, actual usage and reviewer false approvals/rejections. A few initial repeated trials are diagnostic, not statistical proof. Paid trials need separate authorization; this record does not start or schedule them.

Raw traces, conversations, private source excerpts, credentials and personal motivations stay out of this public record. Use bounded aggregates and anonymized regressions.

Provider faults can be a routing choice, not a model or harness defect. Its benefit is a hypothesis until post-release pauses are measured by provider.

A model switch can silently shrink the harness's context. Pi assigns 32,000 tokens to any model missing from its bundled catalog, so choosing a new reviewer model would have started compaction near 24k tokens. Supply the selected model's real window from the live catalog as a fallback only, and keep transport fields compatible with already-published workers during staged rollout.

External review feedback, such as Devin comments on a PR, is untrusted input, not new scope. Give it to a fresh, bounded repair round on the published candidate, rerun the real checks and the independent review, and report requests that fall outside the approved plan instead of implementing them. Human merge consent must name the exact head it approves; any later change invalidates it.

Letting an agent commit moves `HEAD`; any artifact logic that diffs against `HEAD` will silently drop the agent's committed work. Diff against the pinned base. When a sandbox must push, keep the credential away from anything the model controlled: build the pushed commit yourself from the approved files in a fresh repository (a model-controlled git filter can make the committed tree differ from what a check of the workspace index saw), and request the token only for that push.

A reviewer's verdict is not a finding. Collecting every review body as feedback turned Devin's "No Issues Found" into a repair round. Separately, budget repair rounds for their real cost: when the full checks take about 13 minutes, a round that inherits only leftover time fails at the re-review, not at the fix.

An agent that can only report a blocker is not autonomous. When main moved, Chief's only option was to send the owner to GitHub, and its tool text still claimed it could not merge. Give the workflow a self-healing path (rebase by re-implementing the approved plan on current main, with checks and review repeated), keep the model's tool descriptions in step with what the system can do, and never classify a no-op precondition failure as an uncertain write, or the assistant goes silent exactly when the owner asks it to act.

A refusal made before any write must never look like a partial write. On 16 October a `coding_reply` rejected by a revision precondition was recorded as an uncertain write, and the owner-wide guard then refused every write for about two days, while two turns kept retrying it. Measured: 4 refused writes, about 40% of the fortnight's model spend in two turns. The intervention ([journal 92](92-uncertain-coding-write-lock.md)):

- refusals before any write are classified as validation errors;
- an uncertain coding request settles itself only when no record exists under its request key;
- one `WRITE_BLOCKED` refusal blocks the rest of the turn, including re-delegation to another agent.

Independent review then found two inputs that would have settled a call that did write: an upper-case job id compared against canonical uuids, and a `LIKE` pattern broken by a backslash in a key. Devin found that a child agent's block did not reach the coordinator. All three are now regression tests. Not yet observed: a real coding refusal after the release.

A transient fault should not cost the owner hours. Over two weeks, paused coding jobs waited a median of 7 hours for the owner to come back, and some of those pauses needed only another attempt. The host now classifies a pause when it observes it, and retries once after a stopped sandbox, a lost heartbeat or a provider 429 or 5xx ([journal 96](96-coding-transient-retry.md)). The retry is keyed to the attempt, so a second pause waits for the owner. It never runs for a job with a PR, while cleanup is pending, or for work-caused pauses such as an exhausted allocation. Independent review found the key subtlety: a provider fault that recovery already overcame must not make a later, work-caused pause look transient, so the rejection must be the attempt's last model event. Unmeasured: the share of real pauses this covers, and whether retried attempts complete.

Prompt caching depends on the provider, and a passing request says nothing about it. On 19 October synthetic probes showed that Anthropic models cache only when the request is marked. Gemini 4 Argon's implicit cache missed whenever the request tail changed. Gemini Flash cached only with an explicit marker on the opening system message. The shared OpenRouter adapter now adds these markers for coding models too, and the owner-approved $4/$20 ceilings also widen the coding model catalog ([journal 100](100-opus-main-model-and-caching.md)). After any model change, check cached tokens on repeated calls rather than assuming the provider caches.

## Coding attempts move into EC2 boxes — 20 October 2026

The owner decided to close out CodeBuild and run coding on self-managed EC2 boxes ([journal 91](91-boxed-subagents.md#phase-4a-coding-attempts-in-boxes--20-october-2026)). The first step changes where the worker runs, not what it does. Each attempt becomes one task in its own box, behind the same five-method sandbox interface, so approval, review proof, publication and every per-call check stay on the host.

Moving a worker moves its boundary too, and that is where review found the real defect. On CodeBuild the worker is the first process and locks itself down (non-dumpable, subreaper) before reading its token. In a box, a small task program starts the worker. It is the container's main process and runs as the same user as repository commands, and it held the token with neither protection, so a test or install script could have read it through Node's inspector or `/proc`. The task program now refuses to read the task without `--disable-sigusr1` and the native lockdown, and the gateway pins its exact command at startup. Lessons:

- When a credential holder changes, re-derive who shares its user, process tree and namespace.
- Make a rollback safe before the rollout. Switching new attempts back to CodeBuild must not strand an attempt already in a box, including one whose box exists but whose ID was never recorded.
- Measure the new boundary's resources. The worker's clones, `npm ci` and tests do not fit the task container's 512 MB `/tmp`. A cold box also needs minutes before the first heartbeat, which the 180-second loss rule would have treated as a dead worker.

Evidence boundary:

- **Reproduced:** synthetic tests for the box sandbox, routing, rollback, the task program's refusals and a controller-level attempt.
- **Not yet measured:** a real coding job on EC2, and whether boxes change completion, speed or cost.

The first real attempt on EC2 (20 October) planned in a box, and the box was gone 24 seconds after the result. The same session exposed an older defect that had nothing to do with boxes. Cancelling a paused job reopened its long-finished sandbox cleanup, so the owner could not clear old jobs while anything else ran, or more than one at a time. A state flag that feeds a uniqueness constraint must change only when the thing it tracks changes ([journal 91](91-boxed-subagents.md#first-real-coding-attempt-on-ec2--20-october-2026)).

CodeBuild is removed (phase 6, 20 October): coding runs only in EC2 boxes ([journal 91](91-boxed-subagents.md#phase-6-codebuild-removed--20-october-2026)).

**Review defect: a job's snapshot outlived its image.** A job keeps the worker image it was created with through every round: plan approval, rebases, feedback repairs, resumes and retries. Accepting only the current image would have paused every in-flight job at the next image update. CodeBuild had silently absorbed those jobs. The coding box profile now keeps a reviewed list of earlier box-capable images, and each box starts with the job's own image. The re-pin procedure moves the old digest into that list.

**Recovery defect.** A box provider's own record is authoritative, so "not found" now settles cleanup instead of holding the coding queue forever. Startup also recognises an unrecorded launch whose box exists.

Lesson: when one execution path is removed, list what it quietly absorbed (old snapshots, unrecoverable states) and give each a deliberate home.

A synthetic cache probe is only as good as its resemblance to production. The first Opus turns missed their cache on most later calls ([journal 104](104-opus-cache-misses-in-production.md)), which the earlier append-only probes never showed. The hypothesis is that a mid-turn tool load, followed by Chief removing the previous step's reasoning, left no cache entry to reuse; older history shifting within a turn also changed the prefix. The shared adapter now also marks the opening system message and the owner's message, for coding models on Anthropic too. Read cached tokens per call from real turns before trusting a probe.

**Allocation limits that outlived their reason (20 October).** The medium canary used its full 40 minutes and 272 model calls, about $0.26, and saved a ready patch without reaching checks. The 40-minute attempt length had been chosen under CodeBuild's unexplained 45-minute cap, and it was enforced twice: in `config/coding-pi.json`, and again as a hard ceiling in the controller. Boxes have no such cap. With the owner's approval, the schema now allows up to 1,000 model calls and 2,500 tool calls, and the Pi attempt ceiling is 100 minutes, inside the box task's 2-hour deadline. New jobs move to 90 minutes, 800 calls and 2,000 tool calls once the worker image carries the wider schema. Lesson: when a platform constraint goes away, search for every place it was encoded, not just the obvious setting.

**A refusal without a reason cannot be diagnosed (20 October).** The resumed canary paused after 10 minutes. The host refused a model call with the generic `worker_request_rejected` (409) before recording it. The model-slot claim refuses for five different reasons under one message (another call in flight, allocation exhausted, deadline passed, attempt inactive, recovery already used), and the stored rejection said none of them. Each generic refusal now records a fixed reason code. Only the host's own fixed messages are mapped; anything else is `unclassified`, so no free text is stored. The claim also names the condition that refused it. The cause of the canary's pause is still unknown, and the next occurrence will show it.

**A model snapshot is not just a new name (20 October).** The owner asked to move the DeepSeek coder to `deepseek-v4.1-flash-1017`. The worker takes each model's protocol rules from Pi's bundled list by exact ID: reasoning content must be replayed on assistant messages, there is no developer role, and OpenRouter's reasoning format applies. That list predates `-1017`, so a configuration-only switch would have silently dropped those rules, and the native-protocol test then fails the reasoning-content replay. A dated snapshot missing from the list now reuses its base model's metadata. Lesson: check what the runtime derives from a model ID before changing one.

**An owner view is not the stored state (21 October).** `coding_status` knew nothing about the box running an attempt. The obvious read, `boxes.state`, carries eleven values; the owner asked for seven, and five of the stored ones (`launching`, `starting`, `stopping`, `stopped`, plus the difference between `held` and `terminated`) describe the box layer rather than the job. The view maps all eleven into the seven and sends an unknown value to `held`, because that claims neither that the box runs nor that it is gone. It also returns nothing at all — not an error — for a job on an older `chief-coding:` sandbox, a malformed `box:` id or a missing row. Lesson: an owner-facing summary is a translation, so map the stored vocabulary totally, test it against the live CHECK constraint rather than a hand-written list, and let a missing record degrade to no view instead of failing the call ([journal 112](112-coding-status-box-view.md)). Reproduced by synthetic controller tests; a production `coding_status` call against a real boxed job is not yet recorded.

**Protected changes go to the owner's card (21 October).** The first medium job on EC2 (PR 305) touched `src/coding/` and `src/runtime.ts`, so it stopped at manual review: the protected-path rule sent every change to Chief's own controls to GitHub. "Continue" could not lift that hold. In the owner-merge workflow the owner approves every merge on a card anyway, so the owner decided protected PRs should reach that card too, which now names the protected files. The card is the review boundary, not GitHub. Four kinds of path still stop for an operator: database migrations, Compose, environment files and workflows. Each would fail or do harm through Chief's merge: the release guard refuses the first two without a rollout, environment files hold secrets, and the GitHub App cannot merge workflows.

**A tool error must name the real cause (23 October).** Cancelling old coding jobs failed on the single-worker index, but the host mapped every `duplicate key` to "inspect work_status", so Chief blamed an unrelated work task and the owner cancelled it. A cancel that changed nothing returned the job's status like a success, and Chief reported jobs cancelled that were not. Retries of the identical refused call were never capped, because each successful status read reset the guard. The host now reports the refusing index (`CODING_WORKER_BUSY`), fails a no-op cancel with its reason, caps identical refused calls even across reads, and asks for an explicit owner confirmation (`confirmStale`) before restarting coding work idle for more than a day ([journal 114](114-coding-cancel-correctness.md)). Tested on synthetic jobs only; the effect on live turns is unmeasured.

Lesson: when a write changes nothing, say so and why; a status read is not an acknowledgement.

**Fewer coding notices, and a measurement before reusing review (23 October).** A private review of 14–20 October (reported) counted 107 coding notices in 7 days. One two-line README job sent 42, and 24 notices went out at night. PR 292 had made the wording plain, but the count did not change, because the outbox sent every milestone as its own message. Progress now folds into one status message per stretch of work, edited in place. Questions, approval cards, failures and results still arrive separately, so they notify. Progress that would start a new message waits out the owner's quiet hours, and a bulk cancel becomes one summary. The outbox rules hold for edits too: an uncertain edit is never replayed, and the intended text is saved before each edit, so the next one catches up ([journal 115](115-fewer-coding-notices.md)).

The same job spent about 42 minutes of box time on two rebases, each repeating the full review. Skipping review when the rebase reproduces the reviewed artifact byte for byte needs two things: a worker change shipped through the pinned image, and an owner decision. The reviewer saw the diff against the old main, and the review exception binds approval to the exact attempt and tree. So this change only records, per rebased revision, whether the candidate matches. Lessons:

- Notice volume is a delivery property. Fix it where messages are made, not where milestones are named.
- Measure how often a shortcut would apply before changing a review safeguard for it.

Evidence boundary:

- **Tested:** synthetic outbox and rebase tests.
- **Not yet measured:** messages per live job after release, and the share of identical rebases.

**Artifact limits sized for a toy change (29 October).** A coding job for a medium feature (classifying shared links into a study-resources store) implemented for 52 minutes, then paused twice with "Artifact too large". The worker exports each changed file whole, and the repository's rules have every change update `docs/current-work.md` and `docs/releases.md`, about 120 KB each. Those two alone took half of the 500 KB budget, and `releases.md` was about to pass the 128 KB per-file cap, which would have failed every job that touched it. At the owner's request, every bound on the path was raised together: files, total size, upload, model request and tool-argument size, and attempt length and call counts. A limit in one link of the chain (worker export, gateway schema, upload size, GitHub publish) would otherwise just move the failure. Lesson: size limits for the coding pipeline against the repository's real files, not against a typical diff. Review then found exactly that: Pi session entries (one message each) were still capped at 512 KB, so a single large file write would have failed the attempt at session upload. That cap is now 4 MB (one model request), the per-job session allowance is 512 MB, and files over 256 KB are published to GitHub as their own blobs instead of inline in one tree request.

The configuration followed in one change, so a job never pairs the new limits with an older worker. It re-pins the worker image to `sha256:97cee1ae…`, built from `8e53d52`, which carries the raised limits and the 4-hour box task deadline. The image before it moves into the coding profile's `previousImages`. New jobs get 150 minutes, 1,500 model calls and 4,000 tool calls, and the coding box's hard maximum rises from 3 to 6 hours, so a full attempt plus boot fits.

**One reviewer model failing the same step (29 October).** The re-run of that link-classification job implemented for 36 minutes and passed `kite ci`, check, build and format:check. Review then paused it. Gemini 4 Argon's third review call came back empty. On resume, the same step got a 502 and then, on the single in-run retry, another empty reply. OpenRouter's metadata for the 502 shows Google stopped mid-generation: 344 completion tokens, no finish reason, a 34,074-token prompt. Size alone does not explain it: Gemini had reviewed prompts up to 47,487 tokens in the previous two weeks. The cause is unproven. Retries could not help a failure that repeats at one step. The Pi runtime's in-run retry covers only provider 429 and 5xx, and the job-level automatic retry excludes empty replies. The owner moved the coding reviewer to Claude Opus 5.5, for new jobs through configuration and for the paused job by a guarded edit of its pinned settings. Lesson: a reviewer that fails the same step repeatedly is a model problem, not a transient one, so the fix is another model, not another retry.

Evidence boundary:

- **Measured:** three failed reviewer calls on one job, and the OpenRouter metadata of one of them.
- **Hypothesis:** content in this review step that Google's endpoint cannot complete.
- **Not yet measured:** an Opus 5.5 review on a Pi job.

**An approval is about the spec (31 October).** Switching a paused job's reviewer by editing its settings invalidated the owner's approval, because the approval hashed the whole settings block. The owner's point: a plan is a spec, and any agent can implement it. The approval now covers only the objective, context and plan, and a stopped job's models and limits can change on request ([journal 136](136-approval-covers-the-spec.md)). Lesson: before editing stored state directly, find everything derived from it. And bind an approval to what the person actually read, not to everything nearby.
